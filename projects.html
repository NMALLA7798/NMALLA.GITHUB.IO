Project 1

Supplier performance and inventory risk

Where do late and short deliveries come from, and what would it cost to stop them hurting service?

Synthetic data: 24 suppliers, 150 SKUs, 2,722 purchase orders, 104 weeks of demand (Jan 2024 to Dec 2025)

SQL (CTEs, windows) / Python / pandas / scikit-learn / Monte Carlo

What I analyzed

Delivery records for 24 suppliers and 150 SKUs across 2,722 purchase orders and two years of demand. I looked for who delivers late or short, which SKUs matter most, and what a better reorder policy would cost.

Results

  • 73.6% of purchase orders arrive on time and in full. 3 of 24 suppliers fall below 60% and cause 15.4% of late deliveries.
  • 55 of 150 SKUs (36.7%) carry 80.5% of demand value, so they get the tightest inventory controls.
  • A reorder rule that allows for lead-time variability lifts simulated service on those SKUs from 83.8% to 93.9% for 20.3% more inventory ($336K).
  • A trend and seasonality forecast has 30% less error than a naive forecast.

OTIF has not improved in two years

The 3-month average moves between 69.8% and 78.2% from 2024-04 to 2025-12 with no sustained improvement. January 2024 is left out because too few orders had arrived to measure.

55 SKUs make up 80.5% of demand value

Ranking by 2025 demand value puts 55 of 150 SKUs (36.7%) in Class A. Class B takes the next 39 SKUs to reach 95% of value. The last 56 SKUs are 4.9% of value, so they get the lightest controls.

Planning on promised lead times leaves Class A under-protected

I simulated 4,000 replenishment cycles for each of the 55 Class A SKUs, sampling each supplier's real lead-time history. The current rule (demand over the promised lead time plus one week) avoids a stockout in 83.8% of cycles, and in 66.7% for SKUs from at-risk suppliers.

Safety stock built from lead-time and demand variability lifts service to 93.9%. The cost is 20.3% more Class A inventory value ($336K on $1.65M).

Trend and seasonality regression cuts forecast error by 30%

I tested four methods on three rolling 13-week windows. The regression has a weighted absolute percentage error (WAPE) of 35.3% per SKU-week against 50.6% for the naive baseline. On total weekly demand the gap is wider: 5.5% against 21.5%. SKU-level error stays high because demand is lumpy, which is why safety stock still matters.

Recommendations

  1. Put the 3 at-risk suppliers on corrective action. Start with Supplier 18 (Vietnam), which carries 8.1% of spend at 53.8% OTIF. Qualify a second source for its SKUs while the plan runs.
  2. Replace promised-lead-time reorder points on Class A SKUs. Use safety stock built from actual lead-time and demand variability. Begin with the 6 Class A SKUs sourced from at-risk suppliers, where simulated service rises from 66.7% to 93.7%.
  3. Fund the inventory deliberately. The change adds about $336K (20.3%) of Class A inventory. Weigh it against stockout losses and the $195K already spent on expediting.
  4. Run the scorecard every month. The query is in this project. Track the 3-month OTIF average and each supplier's lead-time spread, since spread drives safety stock.

The ABC query

-- ABC / Pareto on 2025 demand value. Class A = SKUs making up the first 80% of value.
WITH s AS (
    SELECT d.sku, p.category, p.supplier_id,
           SUM(d.units * p.unit_cost) AS value
    FROM weekly_demand d JOIN products p USING (sku)
    WHERE d.week_start >= '2025-01-01'
    GROUP BY d.sku, p.category, p.supplier_id
),
r AS (
    SELECT *,
           SUM(value) OVER (ORDER BY value DESC) / SUM(value) OVER ()  AS cum_share,
           value / SUM(value) OVER ()                                   AS own_share,
           ROW_NUMBER() OVER (ORDER BY value DESC)                      AS rnk
    FROM s
)
SELECT sku, category, supplier_id, ROUND(value) AS value, rnk,
       ROUND(100.0 * cum_share, 1) AS cum_pct,
       CASE WHEN cum_share - own_share < 0.80 THEN 'A'
            WHEN cum_share - own_share < 0.95 THEN 'B'
            ELSE 'C' END AS abc_class
FROM r
ORDER BY rnk;

Limits

The dataset is synthetic. It was generated with realistic structure (seasonality, Pareto-shaped demand, a few unreliable suppliers), so the numbers show the method and not real supplier performance. The inventory simulation resamples historical lead times and weekly demand and treats them as independent. Inventory value counts average cycle stock plus safety stock and ignores holding and ordering costs.

Budget vs actual and outlook

Revenue is on plan. Why is EBITDA $2.6M short, and where is the year heading?

Synthetic data: fictional omnichannel company, 4 business units, actuals Jan 2023 to Sep 2026, budget to Dec 2026

SQL (conditional aggregation, LAG) / Python / scikit-learn / bootstrap intervals

What I analyzed

Budget and actual results for four business units from January 2023 to September 2026, focused on the 2026 year-to-date EBITDA shortfall and the outlook for the rest of the year.

Results

  • Revenue is $59.1M (+0.4% vs budget) but EBITDA is $7.3M (-26.0%), a $2.6M shortfall.

Recommendations

  1. Reprice or re-source Retail. Unit costs are running well above the 2% plan and prices have not followed. Retail lost $0.9M to cost and $0.8M to volume, and is at -$0.75M EBITDA YTD against $1.01M budgeted.

The EBITDA bridge query

-- Price / volume / cost / opex decomposition of the EBITDA variance, computed at
-- month x BU grain so the effects sum exactly to the total variance:
--   volume = (units_a - units_b) * (price_b - cost_b)
--   price  = (price_a - price_b) * units_a
--   cost   = -(cost_a - cost_b) * units_a
WITH a AS (SELECT * FROM pnl WHERE scenario = 'Actual'),
     b AS (SELECT * FROM pnl WHERE scenario = 'Budget')
SELECT a.bu,
       SUM((a.units - b.units) * (b.price - b.unit_cost))                    AS volume_effect,
       SUM((a.price - b.price) * a.units)                                    AS price_effect,
       SUM(-(a.unit_cost - b.unit_cost) * a.units)                           AS cost_effect,
       SUM(-(a.marketing - b.marketing))                                     AS marketing_effect,
       SUM(-((a.payroll + a.other_opex) - (b.payroll + b.other_opex)))       AS payroll_other_effect,
       SUM((a.revenue - a.cogs - a.payroll - a.marketing - a.other_opex)
         - (b.revenue - b.cogs - b.payroll - b.marketing - b.other_opex))    AS total_ebitda_var
FROM a JOIN b ON a.month = b.month AND a.bu = b.bu
WHERE a.month BETWEEN '2026-01-01' AND '2026-09-01'
GROUP BY a.bu
ORDER BY a.bu;

Limits

The dataset is synthetic and the company is fictional. The forecast holds gross margin and marketing share at each unit's last six months, runs payroll and other costs at the last three months plus 3% a year, and fits revenue with a trend and yearly seasonality. The 80% interval comes from resampled model residuals, so it covers model error and leaves out shocks such as a lost customer. The tornado moves one driver at a time.

Project 3

Subscription growth, churn and onboarding

Retention looks weak and paid social keeps growing. Where should the business invest next quarter?

Synthetic data: fictional fitness app, 18,283 signups (Jan 2024 to Dec 2025), 14-day trial, three plans, one A/B test

SQL (recursive CTE, set operations) / Python / A/B testing / cohort and funnel analysis

What I analyzed

A fictional fitness app with 18,283 signups over two years, a funnel, subscription records and an onboarding A/B test. I tested why growth economics look weaker and what to do first.

Results

  • The onboarding checklist lifts activation 7.5 points (95% CI 3.9 to 11.1), worth about $36K a year in six-month revenue. Moving budget to search only pays if search holds 84% of its efficiency.

Retention is stable, so churn after payment is not the problem

The first hypothesis was that retention is falling. Cohort retention says otherwise. Three months after the first payment, 82.5% to 90.7% of each cohort is still subscribed. The average is 86.9% for the earlier half of cohorts and 86.8% for the later half.

Rows with fewer columns are recent cohorts that have not had time to age.

Acquisition quality is slipping as paid social grows

Paid social grew from 21.7% of signups in Q1 2024 to 36.8% in Q4 2025, and 7-day activation fell from 43.7% to 36.1%.

Comparing H1 2024 with H1 2025, signup-to-paid conversion slipped from 30.5% to 29.7%. The channel shift explains -0.7 points. Falling conversion inside paid social (25.9% to 23.7%) explains -0.7, and the other channels add +0.5.

Each channel loses customers at a different step

In January to August 2025, 23.3% of paid-social signups activated, against 54.1% for referral. Paid social also keeps the fewest customers to day 90: 19.0% of signups, against 32.6% for referral.

Paid social returns $0.95 of six-month revenue per $1 spent. Referral returns $4.03 and partners $2.39.

Activation is the strongest churn signal

Among customers who started paying by June 2025, 38.5% of those who did not activate in week one cancelled within six months, against 16.8% of activated customers.

Paid-social customers churn at 32.0% and referral customers at 14.6%. Two or more support tickets in the first month also flag risk: 32.1% churn, against 17.9% with none.

The onboarding checklist lifts activation by 7.5 points

I tested the checklist on 2,795 signups from July to September 2025, split 50/50. Activation was 43.8% with the checklist and 36.2% without. Small tests tend to overstate winners, so I plan with the low end of the interval.

Recommendations

  1. Roll out the onboarding checklist to every signup. It is the only initiative with a clear, measured effect, worth about $36K a year, or $19K at the low end of the interval.
  2. Hold paid-social spend flat until its activation recovers. It returns $0.95 per $1 in six months and its customers churn at 32.0%.
  3. Hold off on moving that budget to search. The move only pays if search keeps 84% of its current efficiency at higher spend. This data cannot show that, so test a small shift first.
  4. Pilot referral expansion and early-warning outreach. Run each with a control group and decide on measured results.
  5. Report four KPIs weekly: activation, signup to paid, 90-day retention and return per $1 by channel, using the definitions above.

The cohort retention query

-- Retention by paid-start cohort and months since first payment. A recursive CTE builds the month
-- offsets; a cell is kept only when the whole cohort has been observed that long.
WITH RECURSIVE months(m) AS (
    SELECT 0 UNION ALL SELECT m + 1 FROM months WHERE m < 12
),
paying AS (
    SELECT customer_id, strftime('%Y-%m', paid_start_date) AS cohort, paid_start_date, cancel_date
    FROM customers WHERE converted = 1
)
SELECT p.cohort, m.m AS months_since,
       COUNT(*) AS cohort_size,
       SUM(CASE WHEN p.cancel_date IS NULL
                  OR p.cancel_date > date(p.paid_start_date, '+' || m.m || ' months') THEN 1 ELSE 0 END) AS active
FROM paying p
JOIN months m
WHERE date(p.cohort || '-01', '+1 month', '-1 day', '+' || m.m || ' months') <= '2025-12-31'
GROUP BY p.cohort, m.m
ORDER BY p.cohort, m.m;

Limits

The dataset is synthetic and the app is fictional. Channel costs per signup are assumptions. Business-case figures count only the first six months of revenue per customer and rest on the stated assumptions, so they rank initiatives and are not a forecast. The A/B test assumes the checklist effect is the same across channels, and the planned rollout should be monitored for that.

Project 4

Fleet management: AI-equipped vs conventional trucks

Is the AI premium worth paying? Compare maintenance, operating cost, acquisition cost and profit across a 120-tractor fleet.

Synthetic data: fictional freight carrier, 120 tractors (60 AI, 60 conventional), 3,739 vehicle-months, Oct 2023 to Sep 2026

SQL (CTEs, joins, bucketing) / Python / regression with vehicle-level bootstrap / NPV and payback model

What I analyzed

120 tractors (60 AI-equipped, 60 conventional) over 3,739 vehicle-months and 2,417 maintenance events. I compared acquisition cost, operating cost, maintenance and profit, adjusted for lane and age, and built a model of the buy decision.

Results

  • AI tractors earn $693 more per vehicle-month after adjusting for lane, age and season (95% interval $278 to $1,291).
  • The gain is entirely long-haul (+$2,941). Regional is flat and urban loses $2,967 per vehicle-month.
  • Unplanned breakdowns fall 38% and maintenance cost per mile 16%, but AI maintenance overtakes conventional after about two years as sensors age.
  • The $75,100 premium pays back in 19 months on long-haul. Buying AI for regional or urban lanes loses money over a 7-year life at current prices.

The profit gap holds after adjusting for lane mix and age: $693 a month

The naive gap is $1,030 per vehicle-month, but AI units are newer (19 vs 30 months on average) and 52% of them run long-haul against 40% of conventional units. A regression controlling for lane, age and season gives $693 with a 95% interval of $278 to $1,291 (bootstrapped by vehicle, so one truck's months are not counted as independent).

By lane the picture is stark: long-haul +$2,941, regional +$178 (interval -$76 to $455, so not distinguishable from zero) and urban -$2,967.

Predictive maintenance cuts breakdowns 38%, but repairs cost more per event

AI units log 0.47 unplanned breakdowns per 100,000 miles against 0.76, and lose less time to the shop (6.6 vs 7.5 hours a month). Condition-based servicing also stretches the preventive interval. Offsetting that, corrective repairs average $3,332 against $2,639 because sensors and calibration add parts and labor, and sensor refreshes take 4% of AI maintenance spend.

AI maintenance cost climbs faster with age and overtakes conventional after about two years

For the first two years an AI tractor costs less per mile to maintain ($0.11 to $0.12, against $0.13 and $0.17). From month 24 the sensor and compute refresh cycle pushes it to $0.18 per mile at 24 to 35 months and $0.21 at 36 to 47, above conventional units' $0.15 and $0.19. The 48+ month bucket is hidden for AI because only a handful of vehicle-months exist. Any business case that uses first-year maintenance savings overstates the benefit.

Recommendations

  1. Buy AI-equipped tractors for long-haul only. Payback is 19 months and the 7-year advantage is $139,164 per truck. Hold regional purchases until the premium or software fee falls.
  2. Move or replace the 9 urban AI units. At the fitted gap they cost about $320,486 a year against conventional units. Reassigning them to long-haul lanes is the cheaper fix.
  3. Budget for the sensor refresh. Put an accrual for the month-30 to month-48 refresh into the AI business case; it is 4% of AI maintenance spend and absent from first-year numbers.
  4. Negotiate the software fee. At $1,650 a month it is the single largest AI-specific operating cost; each $500 saved adds $6,000 per truck per year.

The maintenance-by-age query

-- Maintenance cost per mile by vehicle age bucket (age at the time of the cost).
WITH mx AS (SELECT vehicle_id, month, SUM(cost) AS maint_cost FROM maintenance GROUP BY vehicle_id, month)
SELECT v.type,
       CASE WHEN o.age_months < 12 THEN 0 WHEN o.age_months < 24 THEN 1 WHEN o.age_months < 36 THEN 2
            WHEN o.age_months < 48 THEN 3 ELSE 4 END AS bucket,
       COUNT(*) AS vehicle_months, SUM(o.miles) AS miles, SUM(COALESCE(mx.maint_cost, 0)) AS maintenance
FROM monthly_ops o JOIN vehicles v USING (vehicle_id)
LEFT JOIN mx ON mx.vehicle_id = o.vehicle_id AND mx.month = o.month
GROUP BY v.type, bucket;

Limits

The data is synthetic and the carrier is fictional. 'AI' stands for AI-equipped tractors (driver-assist or autonomous-capable with AI predictive maintenance and routing), not fully driverless operation, and the planted effects are assumptions, not industry benchmarks. The payback model holds rates, revenue per mile and diesel flat, uses a straight-line age slope for maintenance, and ignores financing beyond an 8% discount rate, taxes and incentives. The bootstrap resamples vehicles, so it reflects fleet-level noise but not a change in freight market conditions.

How I design charts

The four projects use 22 chart types, each picked for the question it answers. Six rules apply to all of them.

The title states the finding.

Each chart sits under a headline such as "OTIF has not improved in two years", so the reader gets the point before reading the axes.

One color means one thing.

Each business unit keeps its color across the charts that compare units. Orange marks the option or segment that needs attention.

Uncertainty stays visible.

Forecasts carry an 80% interval, the A/B test carries confidence whiskers, and the business case shows a range.

Labels, a legend and a readout.

Multi-series charts have direct labels and a legend, tooltips give exact values, and the controls print the numbers they change.

Honest axes.

Bars start at zero. Lines may start higher, and the axis says so.

Built for any screen.

Charts redraw for light and dark themes, fit a phone width, and the controls work with a keyboard.
